# Burr grinders, explained

Ask ten people what upgrade made the biggest difference to their home coffee and most of them will say the grinder. Not the machine, not the kettle, not the beans. That sounds like folklore until you understand what a grinder is really doing, and why the burr kind does it so much better than the spinning-blade kind.

## What grinding is for

Brewing is extraction: water dissolves flavour compounds out of roasted coffee. Whole beans extract almost nothing, because water only touches the outside. Grinding exposes the inside of the bean, and the finer you grind, the more surface area the water can reach and the faster extraction happens.

The catch is that extraction is not one thing. The first compounds to dissolve are bright acids and fruity aromatics. Sugars and the heavier, rounder flavours come next. Last come the dry, bitter, woody notes you mostly want to leave behind. A good cup stops somewhere in the middle of that curve.

So the goal of grinding is not just "fine" or "coarse". It is **even**. If every particle is roughly the same size, every particle extracts at roughly the same rate, and you can stop the whole batch at the sweet spot.

## Blades versus burrs

A blade grinder is a propeller in a cup. It does not grind so much as shatter. Beans near the blade get hit hundreds of times and turn to dust; beans that bounce to the edge get hit a few times and stay as chunks. The result is a mix of powder and gravel in one dose.

Brew that mix and you get two cups at once. The dust over-extracts and tastes bitter and drying. The chunks under-extract and taste sour and thin. People often describe blade-ground coffee as "harsh but weak", which is exactly that combination.

A burr grinder works differently. Two hardened, ridged surfaces sit a set distance apart, one turning and one fixed. Beans are pulled in, cracked, and then crushed into smaller and smaller pieces until they are small enough to escape through the gap. Because nothing leaves until it fits through the gap, the particle size is controlled by the setting, not by luck.

## Conical and flat burrs

Burr sets come in two main shapes.

- **Conical burrs** use a cone-shaped inner burr turning inside a ring-shaped outer burr. They run at lower speeds, stay cooler, and tend to fit in smaller and cheaper grinders, including most hand grinders.
- **Flat burrs** use two parallel discs, one above the other. Coffee travels outward from the centre and exits at the edge. They usually need a stronger motor and more precise alignment, so they show up in pricier machines.

The taste difference is real but smaller than forums suggest. Conical burrs tend to produce a slightly wider spread of particle sizes, with a few more fines, which can give a heavier, rounder body. Flat burrs tend to produce a tighter spread, which can make flavours more separated and clear. Neither is "correct"; they suit different preferences.

What matters more than the shape is the **build quality around it**. Burrs that wobble, sit off-centre or flex under load will grind unevenly no matter how good the cutting geometry is.

## Fines, boulders and retention

Three terms come up whenever grinders are compared.

1. **Fines** are the very small particles every grinder makes when beans fracture. Some fines help espresso build resistance; too many make filter coffee muddy and slow to drain.
2. **Boulders** are oversized particles that slipped through. They under-extract and add sourness.
3. **Retention** is the coffee left inside the grinder after you finish. Stale grounds from yesterday mixing into today's dose matter a lot for espresso, where you are weighing to a tenth of a gram, and less for a big batch of filter.

## Stepped and stepless adjustment

Stepped grinders click between fixed settings. They are easy to repeat: "setting 14" means the same thing tomorrow. Stepless grinders let you turn to any point, which is useful for espresso, where the right setting may sit between two clicks. For filter brewing, a grinder with reasonably fine steps is plenty.

## What to look for

If you are shopping, focus on a short list.

- **Burr alignment and a stable shaft.** This is where cheap grinders cut corners.
- **The range you actually need.** An espresso-capable grinder needs fine control at the fine end; a filter-only grinder does not.
- **Low retention** if you plan to switch between beans or brew methods often.
- **Ease of cleaning.** Burrs you can remove without tools get cleaned; burrs that need a screwdriver do not.

## The short version

A burr grinder gives you control over particle size, and control over particle size is control over extraction. Pick one that is well built, covers the brew methods you use, and is easy to keep clean. Then spend a week grinding a little finer and a little coarser with the same beans and tasting the results side by side. That week will teach you more than any spec sheet.
