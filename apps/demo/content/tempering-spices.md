# Tempering whole spices in hot oil

Tempering, called tadka, tarka or chhonk in Indian kitchens, means frying whole spices in a few spoonfuls of hot fat for under a minute and pouring the lot over a finished dish. Many of the aroma compounds in spices dissolve in fat rather than water, so the fat picks them up and carries them through the food. A tadka added at the end gives a dal or a bowl of yogurt the strongest, freshest spice flavour you can get, and it takes about two minutes.

## Fat and pan

Use ghee or a neutral oil such as sunflower or peanut oil, 1 to 2 tbsp for four servings. Ghee tastes richer, and because its milk solids have been removed it takes the heat without burning. Butter cannot. Use your smallest pan, or a tadka ladle, so the fat is deep enough to cover the seeds. In a big frying pan the fat spreads too thin and the spices scorch.

## Temperature

Heat the fat over medium heat until it shimmers, about 170 to 180°C (340 to 355°F). Test it with one cumin seed, which should sizzle at once. If nothing happens, the fat is too cool and the spices will soak up oil and taste raw. If the seed darkens within a couple of seconds, take the pan off the heat for a moment before you start.

## Order and timing

Whole seeds go in first, while the fat is hottest, and whatever burns fastest goes in last. Each one tells you when it is time for the next. For a typical dal tadka:

1. **Black mustard seeds**, about 30 seconds. Wait until they pop and crackle, and hold a lid over the pan.
2. **Cumin seeds**, 10 to 15 seconds, until a shade darker and nutty-smelling.
3. **Sliced garlic**, 30 to 60 seconds, until pale gold. Turn the heat down a little first, because the cumin keeps cooking while the garlic colours. Brown garlic tastes bitter.
4. **Whole dried red chillies**, 5 to 10 seconds, until they darken and puff slightly.
5. **Curry leaves**, if you have them, about 5 seconds. Pat them dry first and stand back, because they spit.
6. **A pinch of asafoetida**, 2 to 3 seconds. It burns almost at once, so it always goes in last.

Whole sweet spices such as cinnamon stick, cloves, green cardamom and bay leaves need longer, 30 to 60 seconds, so they go in first when a recipe uses them. Fenugreek seeds are bitter even when cooked well, so use only a few and take them off the heat at reddish brown. Ground spices such as chilli powder burn in seconds. Stir them in off the heat, right before you pour.

## Pouring

The hot pan keeps cooking the spices after it leaves the heat, so pour the tadka over the dish straight away. It should hiss as it lands. Some cooks do it the other way round and tip a ladle of dal into the tadka pan, which picks up every drop of the flavoured fat.

## At the start or at the end

A tadka can also start a dish, with the same spices fried in the pot before the onions go in. That builds a deeper, mellower base. A finishing tadka is louder and more aromatic. Many dals use both.

## Common mistakes

Water makes hot fat spit hard, so dry curry leaves on a towel and keep wet spoons away from the pan. The other mistake is walking away. The whole thing takes two minutes, and half a minute too long turns cumin black and bitter. If that happens, wipe out the pan and start again, because burnt spices flavour the whole pot.

A tadka does not freeze well. Its aroma fades and its fat separates, so freeze dishes plain and make the tadka fresh when you reheat.
