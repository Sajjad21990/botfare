# Pouring your first latte-art heart

The heart is the first pattern almost every barista learns, and for good reason. It uses the same movements as every more complex design, and it is forgiving enough that you will pour a recognisable one within a week of practice. This guide assumes you already have a machine with a steam wand and can pull a decent shot.

## It starts with the milk

Latte art is ninety percent milk texture. The goal is **microfoam**: milk with tiny, invisible bubbles folded all the way through, so it looks like wet paint and pours in one glossy stream.

- **Use cold milk and a cold pitcher.** Cold milk gives you more time to texture before it gets too hot.
- **Fill the pitcher to just below the base of the spout.** For a 350 ml pitcher that is roughly 150 to 180 ml, enough for one flat white.
- **Purge the steam wand** before you start, to clear condensed water.

## Steaming in two stages

1. **Stretching.** Place the wand tip just under the surface, slightly off-centre, and open the steam fully. You should hear a soft, regular tearing sound, like paper being ripped slowly. Lower the pitcher gently as the milk rises, keeping the tip near the surface. Stretch for only 3 to 5 seconds; for a flat white you want the volume to grow by about a quarter.
2. **Texturing.** Raise the pitcher so the tip sinks a centimetre below the surface. The milk should spin in a steady whirlpool, folding the bubbles in. Keep going until the pitcher is too hot to hold comfortably, around 55 to 60 °C.

Turn the steam off before you pull the wand out, wipe it, and purge it again. Then tap the pitcher on the counter once to pop any large bubbles and swirl it until the surface is uniformly glossy. If you see dull foam on top, you stretched too long; pour it off and use the rest.

## Set up the cup

Pull your shot into a wide cup, around 150 to 180 ml. A wide, round bowl makes it much easier to spread the pattern. Give the espresso a quick swirl so the crema is even.

Hold the cup tilted towards the pitcher at about 30 degrees. The tilt brings the surface of the espresso closer to the spout so the pattern can form early.

## The three phases of the pour

**Phase one: the base.** Start pouring from about 8 to 10 cm above the cup, aiming at the centre, in a thin, steady stream. From this height the milk dives under the crema without leaving a mark. Fill the cup about halfway, slowly levelling the cup as you go.

**Phase two: the drop.** Lower the spout until it almost touches the surface, near the centre of the cup, and pour a little faster. A white circle will appear and grow. This is the moment that feels most unnatural: you must get close and commit. Keep the pitcher still and let the circle expand as the cup fills. Gently rock the pitcher side to side if you want the heart to have layers.

**Phase three: the cut-through.** When the cup is nearly full and the circle is the size you want, raise the pitcher a few centimetres to thin the stream, and draw it straight across the circle, towards the far side of the cup. The thin stream drags the near edge of the circle through the middle and forms the point of the heart. Stop pouring as you pass the far edge.

## Common problems

- **No white appears at all.** You are too high or too slow in phase two. Get closer and pour with more confidence.
- **A white blob that will not spread.** The milk is too stiff: too much air in the stretching stage.
- **The pattern smears into brown streaks.** The milk has separated. Swirl the pitcher continuously right up to the pour.
- **The heart is lopsided.** The cut-through wandered. Pick a point on the far rim and pull straight towards it.

## Practising without wasting milk

Use water with one drop of dish soap in the pitcher. It foams and pours a lot like textured milk, so you can practise the steaming sounds and the three-phase pour as many times as you like. Switch back to milk once the motions feel automatic.

Most people pour their first clear heart somewhere between the tenth and thirtieth try. Take a photo of every attempt; you will see the progress long before it feels like progress.
