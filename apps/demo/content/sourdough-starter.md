# Sourdough starter from scratch

A sourdough starter is flour and water colonised by wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria. To make one, mix equal weights of wholemeal flour and water, then each day discard most of it and feed it fresh flour and water, until it reliably doubles within 4 to 8 hours of a feed. At 24 to 26°C (75 to 79°F) that takes 7 to 14 days. You need a scale, a 1 litre jar and a rubber band.

## What you need

- Wholemeal (whole wheat) or whole rye flour for the first week. Whole grain flour carries more of the yeast, bacteria and nutrients that get a starter going.
- Strong white bread flour for later feeds
- Water at about 25°C (77°F). Tap water is fine if you would drink it; otherwise use filtered water.
- A 1 litre (1 quart) glass jar with a loose lid, a spatula and a digital scale

## Day by day

1. **Day 1.** Mix 50 g wholemeal flour with 50 g water in the jar until no dry flour is left. Scrape down the sides, put the rubber band round the jar at the level of the mixture, cover loosely and leave it somewhere warm.
2. **Day 2.** You may see a few bubbles, or nothing. Stir, discard all but 50 g, then add 50 g flour and 50 g water. Mix well and move the rubber band to the new level.
3. **Days 3 and 4.** Many starters bubble up dramatically and smell of cheese or old socks, then go quiet. That early rise comes from bacteria that die off as the mixture turns acidic, not from yeast. It is normal, so keep feeding once a day.
4. **Days 5 to 7.** Switch to half wholemeal, half white flour. Keep 50 g starter and feed it 25 g wholemeal flour, 25 g white flour and 50 g water. Once it rises and falls clearly each day, feed it every 12 hours.
5. **Days 7 to 14.** Keep feeding twice a day. The starter is ready when it doubles within 4 to 8 hours of a feed, has a domed top full of bubbles and smells pleasantly sour, like yogurt or green apples.

Discarding feels wasteful, but it matters. Without it the jar overflows and the microbes outgrow their food, so the starter turns too acidic and slows down. Keep the discard in the fridge for up to a week and use it in pancakes, crackers or flatbreads.

## Keeping it going

At room temperature a starter needs feeding once or twice a day. Most home bakers keep theirs in the fridge instead and feed it once a week. Keep 20 g of starter, add 50 g flour and 50 g water, leave it out for 1 to 2 hours, then refrigerate it. The cold slows the microbes almost to a stop. Before you bake, take it out and give it two feeds 12 hours apart at room temperature, and use it at its peak, when it has roughly doubled and the top is domed.

## When it goes wrong

A layer of grey liquid on top is called hooch. It means the starter is hungry. Pour it off or stir it in, then feed it. A starter that still will not rise after two weeks is usually too cold. Move it somewhere warmer, such as an oven with only the light on, and check the oven stays below 30°C (86°F). Pink or orange streaks or fuzzy mould mean unwanted microbes have taken over. Throw it away, wash the jar and start again.

## Your first bake

Bake with the starter at its peak, a few hours after a feed. A plain first loaf uses 100 g starter, 500 g strong white flour, 350 g water and 10 g salt, and how much it rises tells you how strong your starter is.
